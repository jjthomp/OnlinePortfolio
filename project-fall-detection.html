Overview

A sensor-agnostic ML pipeline for real-time fall detection in long-term care

Falls are a critical safety concern for older adults, particularly those in long-term care facilities where the risk of serious injury is much higher than in the general population. My objective for this project was to develop a reliable, sensor-independent machine learning model capable of detecting both falls and near-falls using Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) data.

By designing a system that can be integrated into everyday items like smartwatches, jewelry, or life-alert necklaces, I aimed to provide a non-intrusive safety net that ensures caregivers are immediately alerted when a fall occurs, which would improve clinical response times and patient outcomes.

Technical Challenge

Maximizing sensitivity across multiple sensor types

The main challenge with fall detection is that falls are rare events, and the data from everyday activities can look similar to a fall — especially near-falls, where a person loses balance but recovers without hitting the ground. The model needs to be sensitive enough to catch every real fall while not triggering on normal movement.

A second challenge was making the model work across different sensor placements and hardware. In a real care setting, you cannot guarantee that every resident will wear a sensor in the same location, so the model needed to work reliably whether the IMU is on the thigh, the back, or somewhere else. This required building a pipeline that could handle multiple sensor modalities and still produce a consistent prediction.

Signal Processing

Filtering and windowing the IMU data

The first step in the pipeline was preprocessing the raw IMU data. I applied a 0.1 Hz high-pass filter to remove the DC offset caused by gravity, which varies depending on how the device is oriented on the body. I then applied targeted low-pass filters — 150 Hz for linear acceleration and 250 Hz for gyroscope data — to reduce noise while preserving the motion frequencies that are relevant to fall detection.

The filtered data was segmented into 10-second rolling windows with a 5-second overlap. I chose this window size specifically to capture the entirety of a potential fall, which based on the literature lasts approximately 6 seconds from onset to ground contact. The 5-second overlap ensures that a fall occurring near the edge of a window is still fully captured in the adjacent window, which avoids missing events at window boundaries.

Model Development

Random Forest classifier with GridSearch optimization

For the classification stage, I evaluated several models and determined that a Random Forest classifier provided the highest accuracy. Rather than relying on trial and error for the hyperparameters, I used a GridSearch approach with 5-fold cross-validation to systematically determine the optimal settings for the model. This gave me a principled way to compare configurations and avoid overfitting to the training data.

To maximize safety, I implemented a decision strategy where the entire system predicts a fall if even a single sensor identifies one. This means I trained separate models for each sensor location — right thigh, back, and left thigh — and fused their outputs so that any one of them triggering a fall prediction counts as a fall. This prioritizes sensitivity and ensures no fall goes undetected, which is the most important outcome for this type of system.

The training data also included Gold Standard Sensor (GSS) and ECG data alongside the IMU signals. Including these additional modalities gave the model more information to work with during training, which helped reduce false positives during high-exertion activities that can produce IMU signatures similar to falls.
